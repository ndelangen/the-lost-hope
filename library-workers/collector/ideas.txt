# arc design | original
Plot the aftermath at three time scales | Before a major choice, write changes the party could see by nightfall, hear about a week later, and deal with next season. Tie each to a different person with the ability to act. Move only the consequences supported by what actually happened.
Make the opposition reclaim something it lost | Give a defeated opponent one thing they can realistically recover, such as a collaborator, a charter, or a safe route. Introduce a means of recovery the party can notice. If the players offer compensation, let recovery become a negotiation instead of an inevitable rematch.
Make the promising route close for a known reason | Give the party a useful route and say exactly when it closes, such as the last tide before a ferry crosses. A second route opens after that moment with its own cost. Either timetable leads to actionable information, so lateness changes the situation without ending the inquiry.
Give each coalition partner a different deadline | Set one common goal and give each partner a specific last moment to act. Put at least two deadlines on the table before asking for a commitment. A party that cannot help both can still negotiate a delay or offer a narrower promise.
Let a witness keep their reason for staying | Write one thing a witness fears and one concrete reason they have stayed nearby despite it. A safe escort can address the first. Helping their dependents or protecting their livelihood addresses the second. A convincing plan earns cooperation even if they never agree to leave.
Prepare the adversary's explanation after a setback | Write what the opponent tells their own followers if the party interrupts the plan. Make the explanation testable through one visible lie or omission. Players who expose it can change what the followers will do next, even when the opponent escapes.

# facilitation | original
Ask for the result before the method | Before resolving an unusual action, ask what the player wants to be true afterward. If their proposed method would accomplish something else, explain the difference before they commit. An apparently bad tactic may be a good answer to a goal the table had missed.
Hand the next decision to someone outside the exchange | When a long exchange resolves, give the next actionable question to a player who was listening. Ask who they want nearby or what cost they think the group should accept. Join the answer to the scene already in progress so their choice matters immediately.
Signal the next consequence in plain language | If a dangerous detail has escaped notice, summarize what the characters can already see and what will happen if no one acts. Ask for a choice before calling for a roll. Use this when an avoidable setback would otherwise come from a misunderstanding of the scene.
Offer the same clue through an action of another kind | Assign one clue to two approaches with different costs. Watching a crew may take an hour, while helping them lifts the delay but exposes the helper to questions. If the group has already learned the fact, reward the second approach with context instead of repeating the clue.
Reopen a quiet scene with a question from the fiction | If the table stalls, have someone in the scene ask a question whose answer must change their next action. A porter can ask which passenger boards before the crossing closes. Use the answer to move the scene even if no one reaches for a die.
Mark the detail the players can carry forward | When a scene has many useful details, restate one concrete clue the characters can record, point to, or show to someone else. Let the players choose which other detail they care to preserve. Keep separate notes for a clue and for a theory about it.
